Scope note: research conducted 26 Oct 2026 via web search and fetch (about 16 tool calls). Many findings come from abstracts, search snippets or secondary summaries, not full texts. These are marked [abstract/snippet]. Items marked [UNVERIFIED] need checking against the primary source before the final report uses them.

## 1. PSS and PPGIS literature, usability/impact evidence, and the PSS "implementation gap"

### Takeaway
Thirty years of PSS research show a persistent supply-demand mismatch: many tools, little routine use. The reasons are tools that do not fit planners' tasks, and no feedback loop from practice into design. PPGIS (Sieber; Brown & Kyttä; Kahila-Tani/Kyttä in Helsinki) reaches more people and new groups. Its data-quality and representativeness problems remain under-assessed, and evidence of real influence on decisions is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Geertman (2017) describes PSS research as long dominated by a supply-demand discrepancy: practitioners are hesitant to buy, implement or apply PSS even as tools multiply. Published in Transportation Research Part A 104:70-76. — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transa/v104y2017icp70-76.html) [abstract/snippet]
- Vonk & Geertman (2008) call this a "vicious circle" caused by the mismatch between supply and demand. Earlier PSS were general-purpose tools poorly suited to planners' specific needs. — [Springer PDF, Applied Spatial Analysis and Policy](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12061-008-9011-7.pdf) [abstract/snippet]
- Vonk et al. (2005): PSS do not reach maturity because of a slow product life cycle. This cycle lacks an information feedback loop from real-world use, which is a cybernetic argument in itself. — [Univ. Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/limitations-and-potential-of-planning-support-systems-application/) [abstract/snippet]
- In a three-case study of PSS implementation, all three attempts were failures or at best partial successes. — [IGI Global article preview](https://igi-global.com/article/investigating-planning-practice/70082) [abstract/snippet]
- Jiang, Geertman & Witte (2020), "Avoiding the planning support system pitfalls? What smart governance can learn from the PSS implementation gap", extends the argument to smart-city governance. — URL not retrieved [UNVERIFIED citation details; likely Environment and Planning B, 2020]
- "Planning support theatres" and City Analytics Labs are proposed as ways to embed PSS in practice. One 2020 study notes there is insufficient knowledge on how to bridge the gap. — [Utrecht repository record](https://utrecht-main-test.atmire.com/items/497cceb0-6f5c-4a34-9432-117fe5b709f5) [abstract/snippet]
- Sieber (2006), Annals of the AAG 96(3):491-507, is the canonical PPGIS review. It defines PPGIS as GIS used to broaden public involvement in policymaking and to advance grassroots/NGO goals. It argues PPGIS is socially constructed across disciplines and sectors. It sets four evaluation themes: place and people; technology and data; process; outcome and evaluation. — [Wiley/T&F DOI page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1467-8306.2006.00702.x); [full-text copy](https://dusk.geo.orst.edu/virtual/2007/sieber2006.pdf)
- Commentators on Sieber note her doubt that PPGIS can be generalized, because it is highly context-dependent. — [McGill GeoThink blog](https://rose.geog.mcgill.ca/wordpress/?p=4910)
- Brown's research agenda, as summarized in a 2020 tribute: improve participation rates; identify and control threats to spatial data quality; move PPGIS from participation tool to political force; build practitioner capacity. — [Aalto repository, Raymond, Fagerholm & Kyttä 2020](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/192f21be-1312-47f9-ab04-ceff0efa68ba)
- Brown & Kyttä (2018), "Key issues and priorities in participatory mapping: Toward integration or increased specialization?", Applied Geography. — [Aalto research portal](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/key-issues-and-priorities-in-participatory-mapping-toward-integra/) [abstract only; the earlier Brown & Kyttä 2014 "Key issues and research priorities for PPGIS" was not retrieved]
- For PPGIS, the relevance, representativeness and credibility of participants matter more than spatial accuracy, so sampling design is critical. Mapping effort (time spent, markers placed) is a proposed quality proxy. — [Maptionnaire research blog, pros and cons of PPGIS](https://maptionnaire.com/blog/pros-cons-of-ppgis-in-urban-planning) [vendor-affiliated source]
- Simola et al. (2026, Cartography and GIS) find PPGIS data quality is rarely assessed systematically. Geometric precision is testable. Thematic accuracy is not, because the data are subjective. Mapping-effort metadata is often missing. — [Univ. Turku record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/515546763?lang=en_US) [abstract/snippet]
- Helsinki master plan: Kahila-Tani, Broberg, Kyttä & Tyger (2016), Planning Practice & Research 31(2):195-214. PPGIS can support representativeness, independence, early involvement, influence and transparency. Planners and residents need better understanding of its benefits. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/cpprxx/v31y2016i2p195-214.html) [abstract]
- Helsinki survey size: 4,681 visitors, of whom 938 answered nothing. One deck reports 3,745 respondents and nearly 33,000 place markings. Maptionnaire cites about 4,700. The differences come from counting rules. — [T&F record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02697459.2015.1104203); [FAO/Landnet deck](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/user_upload/reu/europe/documents/events2018/landnet11/5.4.pdf); [Maptionnaire Helsinki case](https://www.maptionnaire.com/customer-stories/helsinki-community-engagement-tool) [vendor]
- Maptionnaire claims much of the Helsinki input came from usually hard-to-reach groups (parents, youth). A Helsinki children's survey drew 1,100 respondents. — [Maptionnaire Helsinki case](https://www.maptionnaire.com/customer-stories/helsinki-community-engagement-tool); [Kyttä slides](https://sisu.ut.ee/wp-content/uploads/sites/135/marketta-kytta.pdf) [vendor/presentation; not a measured comparison with census]

### Inferences
- The Milan platform should be co-designed with the planners who will use its outputs, such as the municipal housing department. It should also log how outputs were used, to close the feedback loop that Vonk et al. found missing. The PSS literature implies that adoption, not technical sophistication, is the binding constraint.
- Sieber's four themes (people/place, technology/data, process, outcome/evaluation) give a ready evaluation framework for the project.
- Use Maptionnaire-style "mapping effort" metadata (time, markers, completion) as a data-quality layer. Do not treat raw counts as a mandate.

### Gaps
- No quantitative meta-analysis of PSS usability or impact was found. Usability evidence is mostly case-based.
- Full text of Brown & Kyttä 2014 and of Jiang, Geertman & Witte 2020 was not retrieved. The latter's exact venue is unverified.
- No representativeness analysis of the Helsinki survey against census data was found in the retrieved material.

## 2. Participatory / collaborative MCDA: eliciting and aggregating many stakeholders' weights; showing trade-offs and sensitivity

### Takeaway
Group AHP with the geometric mean is the default in participatory GIS-MCDA. It has documented theoretical weaknesses: it can fail Pareto and non-dictatorship tests, and it behaves poorly when judges disagree strongly. Weight-elicitation methods are not interchangeable. Direct rating and Max100 are more reliable and better liked than point allocation. SWING is more stable over time but harder to use. Aggregation is best used as a learning and deliberation tool, not as an oracle.

### Cited Findings
- In GIS-MCDA for groups, you can aggregate individual preferences before the spatial analysis, or run separate analyses and aggregate the results afterwards. Group AHP/ANP is the most common approach in spatial participatory decisions and can serve as a consensus-building tool. — [Springer chapter, GIS-MCDA group decisions](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-540-74757-4_8) [snippet; mirror URL]
- The geometric mean was recommended for aggregating AHP priorities without solid justification, and the arithmetic mean is more common in practice. Only weight ratios carry meaning, so averaging the pairwise ratios is proposed instead of averaging priority vectors. — [arXiv 2304.08859, "Aggregation and dispersion fallacies in group MCDM"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.08859)
- A Delhi urban-transport case tested group AHP methods against social-choice axioms. The geometric mean method, the most widely used, failed Pareto optimality and non-dictatorship. — [EABER paper](https://eaber.org/publications/application-of-analytic-hierarchy-process-to-prioritize-urban-transport-options-comparative-analysis-of-group-aggregation-methods) [abstract/snippet]
- Scala et al. (2016): the geometric mean can violate AHP principles when judges disagree strongly. They propose a PCA-based weighted geometric mean for that case. — [Scala et al. 2016 accepted manuscript](https://www.drnataliescala.com/s/Scala-et-al-2016-AAM.pdf)
- Expert Choice/Comparion documentation separates aggregation of individual judgments (AIJ) from aggregation of individual priorities (AIP). AIJ suits a group acting as one unit. AIP suits individuals acting separately. — [Comparion help, AIJ and AIP](https://comparion.knowledgeowl.com/help/aij-and-aip) [vendor documentation]
- In participatory forest planning, extended goal programming, the weighted arithmetic mean and the geometric mean gave different rankings. The authors conclude the method should fit the context, and aggregation works better as a learning tool than as a source of an "optimal" answer. — [Open Forest Science Journal 5:23](https://benthamopen.com/DOWNLOAD-PDF/TOFSCIJ-5-23)
- Bottomley, Doyle & Green (2000, J. Marketing Research): direct rating and point allocation (budget allocation) give quite different weight profiles. Direct rating has higher test-retest reliability, and participants prefer it. In simulated choices the same option was picked 88% of the time with direct rating versus 74% with point allocation. — [UTB catalogue record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c389t-954946cfd02d9832eb0deb821881d11a45f5f575ec7fa39db492c67432ed93fb3) [abstract]
- Bottomley & Doyle (2001, Omega): Max100 (anchor the most important criterion at 100) is somewhat more reliable than direct rating. Min10 is clearly less reliable, and participants prefer Max100 and direct rating. — [Cardiff ORCA](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/2576) [abstract]
- Lienert et al. (2016, EJOR), Swiss wastewater stakeholders: SWING gave more stable weights over time than SMART/SWING but was slightly harder for participants. — [IDEAS/RePEc EJOR 253(3)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v253y2016i3p746-760.html) [abstract]
- Danielson & Ekenberg argue that demanding numeric precision in elicitation is problematic. They propose CAR (Cardinal Ranking), a ranking-plus-strength method tested in a large user study. — [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/83641597.pdf)
- A review for low- and middle-income settings calls direct rating the simplest method, with no explicit trade-offs. Swing weighting starts from a hypothetical worst case. — [CORE PDF review](https://core.ac.uk/download/199459246.pdf)
- P-SWING (2019, Knowledge-Based Systems) refines swing to improve decision makers' understanding of the process. — [Datalearner record](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0950-7051/volumes-and-issues/286/paper-detail/8289) [abstract]

### Inferences
- For lay Milan residents, offer a ranking-first interface (CAR-style rank, then adjust strength) or Max100 sliders as the default. Offer pairwise AHP only as an optional "expert mode". With n criteria, AHP needs n(n-1)/2 comparisons, for example 21 for 7 criteria, which is heavy for casual users. (The arithmetic is mine; the burden claim is inferred, not sourced.)
- Swing weighting matters because it ties weights to the actual ranges across candidate sites, such as worst versus best transit access among the real lots. That avoids "importance in the abstract". Use it in a deliberative or second-round module, where its difficulty is acceptable.
- Aggregation: report the distribution of weights (median, interquartile range, clusters), not only a geometric-mean point estimate. Compute the site ranking under each cluster's weights and under the overall median. Show rank stability, for example the share of resampled weight vectors under which each site ranks first. This follows from the dispersion critique (Scala; arXiv 2304.08859) and the "learning tool" conclusion.
- Sensitivity for lay users: use "what would have to change for site B to beat site A" statements plus a weight-slider live ranking. This is a design inference; no user study on lay comprehension of sensitivity displays was found.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares elicitation or aggregation methods with lay citizens in an urban-planning web PPGIS-MCDA. Most evidence uses students, consumers or organized stakeholders.
- No empirical source was retrieved on visualizing trade-offs and sensitivity for non-experts, such as Pareto plots or rank-acceptability indices (SMAA). This needs a targeted search.
- Consensus measures beyond the geometric mean, such as Aguarón's consensus indicators or the Geometric Consistency Index, were not retrieved.

## 3. Civic tech platforms: Decidim, CONSUL/Decide Madrid, Maptionnaire, vTaiwan/Polis, PB, and co-design tools (games, Block'hood/Common'hood, CityScope)

### Takeaway
The flagship platforms show high early engagement but uneven conversion into decisions. Credibility rests on the institution committing to act on outputs (Barcelona PAM) or not (Decide Madrid, where transparency was weak). Polis offers an opinion-mapping and bridging-consensus design suited to surfacing shared ground, but its clustering is contested. Tangible and game-based tools (CityScope FindingPlaces, Common'hood) show that co-design can produce siting outcomes, although with low conversion to construction. Milan already runs Decidim (Milano Partecipa).

## 4. Representativeness: including the "middle of the distribution"

### Takeaway
Self-selected online participation is consistently skewed toward resource-rich residents (more educated, higher income, often male and younger). Remedies with evidence are personalized or random invitations, which raise turnout 4-7x, targeted outreach, and representative deliberative processes based on civic lottery (OECD: about 289 cases). There is some evidence that skewed pools can still match wider preferences (Brazil), but this cannot be assumed.

### Cited Findings
- PB Chicago voters were more often white, college-educated and higher-income than both the local population and politically active residents. — [MetaTOC summary of Sociological Forum article](https://metatoc.com/papers/114039-beyond-the-usual-suspects-reimagining-democracy-with-participatory-budgeting-in-chicago) [abstract]
- Rio Grande do Sul PB (2014): online and offline voters differed demographically, yet support for each budget item correlated strongly. Online voting raised turnout by 8.2%. Technology tends to engage people who are younger, male, higher-income, more educated and heavier social-media users. — [World Bank Open Knowledge record](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/signposting/describedby/0db1e790-e488-5a9c-9e8c-4384aac4105f); [Typeset PDF, "Effects of the internet on participation"](https://typeset.io/pdf/effects-of-the-internet-on-participation-study-of-a-public-z3t08rjc9z.pdf) [abstract]
- A German field experiment found online participants significantly biased toward resource-rich individuals. Personalized invitations raised participation 4-7x and brought in some under-represented people, but only for low-intensity engagement. — [Springer, Z. Politikwissenschaft, DOI 10.1007/s41358-020-00222-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41358-020-00222-7) [abstract]
- Online PB turnout is sometimes at or below 5% of eligible voters. Gelauff et al. (2020) and Shen et al. (2021) study targeted advertising for demographically balanced participation. — [NSF PAR paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10686392) [snippet]
- OECD (2020), "Innovative Citizen Participation and New Democratic Institutions: Catching the Deliberative Wave": about 289 representative deliberative processes (282 analyzed), 1986 to Oct 2019. 52% were at local level. Citizens' juries/panels are the most common (115, 41%). Inclusion criteria: commissioned by a public authority, random selection with demographic stratification, at least one full day of face-to-face deliberation. — [OECD report page](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/innovative-citizen-participation-and-new-democratic-institutions_339306da-en.html); [OECD ch. 3](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/innovative-citizen-participation-and-new-democratic-institutions_339306da-en/full-report/component-7.html)
- Polis documentation discusses combining Polis with lottery-selected assemblies. — [compdemocracy.org, lottery-selected assemblies](https://compdemocracy.org/polis/book/lottery-selected-assemblies/) [not read in full]
- Trust-building and language and communication barriers matter for marginalized groups in participatory GIS. — [SustainabilityMethods wiki, Participatory GIS](https://sustainabilitymethods.org/index.php/Participatory_GIS) [secondary/wiki]

### Inferences
- Use a two-channel design. (a) An open channel: anyone on Milano Partecipa, weighted after the fact. (b) A representative panel: invitations to a stratified random sample from the anagrafe (resident register), by municipio, age, household type and tenure. Report both results side by side, and flag where they diverge.
- Post-stratification: reweight open-channel responses to ISTAT census margins (age, sex, education, household size, tenure, municipio) with raking. Report effective sample size and design effect, and never present the weighted result alone. This is standard survey methodology; no planning-specific source was retrieved.
- Represent potential social-housing tenants explicitly, such as households on ALER/MM waiting lists. The "middle of the distribution" of all residents is not the same as the needs of the target population. This is a normative tension the platform must surface.
- Use a mini-public (citizens' panel, OECD-model) to deliberate on the final weight set and the trade-offs, informed by the open-channel and panel data. That closes the deliberative step of the loop.

### Gaps
- No Italian or Milan-specific data on the digital divide or participation bias in Decidim processes was found.
- No source was retrieved on using census raking specifically for PPGIS or MCDA weights. This is a methodological gap to flag; Brown has some work on PPGIS sampling that was not retrieved.

## 5. Privacy and data protection (GDPR) and algorithmic transparency (EU AI Act) for public bodies

### Takeaway
A site-selection MCDA that ranks lots is not, on the retrieved text, an Annex III high-risk AI system. Annex III does not list site selection or urban planning. Any module that assesses individual households' eligibility or priority for social housing would fall under Annex III point 5(a). The high-risk deadlines were pushed by the Digital Omnibus (provisional deal 7 May 2026: stand-alone Annex III obligations to 2 Dec 2027). Final Official Journal publication is unverified. Household-needs data (health, disability, income, family) triggers strict GDPR handling.

### Cited Findings
- AI Act Annex III point 5(a): AI systems used by or for public authorities "to evaluate the eligibility of natural persons for essential public assistance benefits and services", including granting, reducing, revoking or reclaiming them, are high-risk. Annex III does not list site selection or urban planning. — [artificialintelligenceact.eu, Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)
- Article 49: before using an Annex III high-risk system (except point 2), public-authority deployers must register themselves, select the system and register its use in the EU database. — [EC AI Act Service Desk, Art. 49](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-49)
- Digital Omnibus on AI: provisional agreement 7 May 2026. Stand-alone Annex III high-risk obligations moved to 2 Dec 2027, Annex I embedded systems to 2 Aug 2028. Parliament voted 16 Jun 2026; Council adoption and OJ publication were pending at the time of the sources. — [Gibson Dunn alert](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/); [Sidley Data Matters, 22 Jun 2026](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/06/22/eu-lawmakers-reach-provisional-agreement-to-delay-key-eu-ai-act-obligations/) [UNVERIFIED: final adoption/OJ status as of Oct 2026]
- Article 50 transparency timing is contested between sources. One says synthetic-content transparency applies from 2 Dec 2026. Gibson Dunn says Art. 50 largely keeps its original schedule (2 Aug 2026). — [Airia summary](https://airia.com/the-eu-ai-act-just-got-an-extension-heres-how-to-use-it/); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/) [CONFLICTING]
- The Netherlands keeps a public national algorithm register listing municipal and government algorithms, including a Polis deployment. This is a voluntary transparency model beyond AI Act minimums. — [algoritmes.overheid.nl](https://algoritmes.overheid.nl/en/algoritme/71477174)

### Inferences
- Architectural separation: keep (a) the public site-comparison and weighting tool, which handles anonymous or pseudonymous preferences, out of AI Act high-risk scope and minimize its personal data. Keep (b) any household-eligibility or allocation scoring, which is high-risk under point 5(a) and also within GDPR Art. 22, in a separate, governed system.
- Collect "needs" as coarse categories (household size band, presence of a person with reduced mobility as a yes/no, municipio of residence), not identifiable detail. Use explicit consent for any Art. 9 data, run a DPIA, publish aggregates only with a minimum cell size such as k ≥ 10. The k threshold is my design inference.
- The MCDA model is transparent by construction: weights, normalization and scores are all inspectable. Publish the model card, the weights and the code, and register the tool voluntarily in an algorithm register in the style of the Dutch one, even if this is not legally required.
- Polis-style clustering and any ML used to summarize comments should be disclosed. Article 50 may apply if generative AI produces public-facing text; timing needs verification.
